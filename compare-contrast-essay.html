Traditional Farming vs Modern Agriculture in Rwanda

Rwanda is a small country, but agriculture is the backbone of its economy. About 70% of the population depends on farming for their livelihood (National Institute of Statistics of Rwanda, 2022). For most of Rwanda's history, farmers have used traditional methods passed down through generations. But in the last two decades, the government has pushed hard for modern agricultural practices. Both traditional and modern farming aim to feed families and generate income, but they differ in their methods, productivity, and impact on the environment. Comparing these two approaches helps us understand why the shift to modern agriculture is happening and what is being lost in the process.

Traditional farming and modern agriculture share the same basic goal: growing crops and raising animals to feed people and earn a living. In both systems, farmers depend on the land, the weather, and their own labor. Both types of farmers in Rwanda grow similar staple crops like beans, maize, cassava, and Irish potatoes. They also both face the same challenges of unpredictable rainfall and limited land. According to the Food and Agriculture Organization (2021), smallholder farmers produce about 80% of the food consumed in Sub-Saharan Africa, whether they use traditional or modern techniques. So at the core, both approaches serve the same purpose of food production and family survival.

The first major difference is in the tools and techniques used. Traditional farmers rely on hand hoes, family labor, and local seed varieties that have been grown for decades. They rotate crops based on knowledge passed from their parents and grandparents. Planting and harvesting follow the rainy seasons, and most decisions are based on experience rather than data. Modern agriculture, on the other hand, uses improved seed varieties, chemical fertilizers, and sometimes machinery like small tractors. The Rwandan government's Crop Intensification Program encourages farmers to use fertilizer and certified seeds to increase their yields (Kathiresan, 2012). My grandfather farms in Nyamagabe and he still uses a hand hoe and saves seeds from last season. My uncle, who farms nearby, joined the government program and now buys seeds from the cooperative. They grow the same crops, but my uncle usually harvests more per hectare.

The second major difference is in productivity and market access. Traditional farming produces enough for the family to eat, but there is rarely a large surplus to sell. Modern farming, because of better seeds and fertilizer, tends to produce higher yields that farmers can sell at local markets or to cooperatives. The World Bank (2019) reported that agricultural productivity in Rwanda grew by about 6% per year between 2007 and 2018, largely because of the adoption of modern inputs. But this does not mean traditional farming is a failure. Traditional methods are cheaper because you do not have to buy fertilizer or certified seeds every season. For a farmer who has no savings and cannot get a loan, traditional farming is the only realistic option. The cost of modern inputs is a real barrier for many rural families.

There is also a difference in environmental impact. Traditional farming tends to be better for the soil in the long run because it uses natural composting, intercropping, and crop rotation that maintain soil health. Modern agriculture relies on chemical fertilizers that boost yields in the short term but can degrade the soil over time if used incorrectly. The Food and Agriculture Organization (2021) warns that heavy fertilizer use without proper soil management can lead to nutrient imbalance and water pollution. In Rwanda, where the land is hilly and erosion is already a problem, this is a serious concern. Some farmers are now trying to combine both approaches by using improved seeds with organic composting, which gives better yields without depending entirely on chemicals.

Both traditional and modern farming have a place in Rwanda's future. Traditional methods carry valuable local knowledge and are affordable for the poorest farmers. Modern agriculture produces more food and connects farmers to markets. The best path forward is probably a mix of both, where farmers use improved techniques without abandoning the practices that have kept the soil healthy for generations. As Rwanda's population grows, finding this balance will be important for feeding everyone without destroying the land that feeds them.
