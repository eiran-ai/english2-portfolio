Problem Solution Essay

Addressing Youth Unemployment in Rwanda

Rwanda has one of the youngest populations in the world. Over 60% of Rwandans are under the age of 25 (National Institute of Statistics of Rwanda, 2023). That sounds like an advantage, and it can be, but only if those young people have jobs. Right now, many of them do not. Youth unemployment is one of the biggest challenges facing Rwanda today, and it affects not just the individuals who cannot find work but also families and communities that depend on young people to contribute economically. The problem is complex, but there are practical solutions that could help if they are implemented seriously.

The core of the problem is a mismatch between what the education system produces and what the job market needs. Every year, thousands of young people graduate from secondary school and university, but many of them have qualifications that do not match available jobs. Rwanda's economy needs skilled workers in areas like construction, technology, hospitality, and agriculture, but most graduates have studied general subjects and lack hands-on technical skills. The International Labour Organization (2020) found that in many African countries, the gap between education outcomes and labor market demands is a major cause of youth unemployment. In Rwanda, you can see this clearly. A lot of my classmates from secondary school are still looking for work two years after finishing, even though they have diplomas. The problem is not that they are lazy. The problem is that their diplomas did not teach them something specific enough that an employer needs.

The first solution is expanding technical and vocational training. Rwanda has started doing this through TVET (Technical and Vocational Education and Training) programs, but the scale is still too small and the quality varies. The government should increase funding for TVET schools and make sure their programs are connected to real industries. For example, if Kigali's construction sector needs electricians and plumbers, the TVET schools in the area should train for those exact jobs, with internships at construction companies as part of the program. According to the African Development Bank (2020), countries that invested in demand-driven vocational training saw faster reductions in youth unemployment because graduates were immediately employable. Rwanda's Workforce Development Authority has made progress, but more partnerships with private companies could make the training more relevant and the graduates more competitive.

The second solution is supporting young entrepreneurs instead of only focusing on formal employment. Not every young person needs a salaried job. Some of them have business ideas but lack the capital, skills, and connections to get started. The government and NGOs should expand access to small loans, business training, and mentorship programs specifically designed for young people aged 18 to 30. The Rwandan government already has programs like Hanga Pitchfest, which gives prizes to young entrepreneurs with innovative ideas. But most young people in rural areas have never heard of these programs. Taking them to the district level and advertising them through local radio and community meetings could reach more people. Filmer and Fox (2014) argue that in countries with fast-growing youth populations, self-employment and small business development are more realistic paths out of poverty than waiting for formal sector jobs to appear.

The third solution is investing in the digital economy. Rwanda has positioned itself as a technology hub in East Africa, with initiatives like the Kigali Innovation City project and smart classrooms in schools. But most of these benefits stay in Kigali. Training young people across the country in basic digital skills like web development, data entry, graphic design, and digital marketing could open up remote work opportunities that do not require living in the capital. The World Bank (2019) noted that the digital economy can create jobs faster than traditional industries, especially for young people who are quick to learn new technology. If a young person in Huye can learn to build websites or manage social media accounts, they can work for clients anywhere in the world without moving to Kigali.

Youth unemployment in Rwanda is not going to be solved overnight. But expanding vocational training, supporting young entrepreneurs, and investing in digital skills are three concrete steps that could make a real difference. Rwanda has the advantage of having a young, motivated population and a government that takes development seriously. The challenge is making sure that the energy and ambition of young people are matched with real opportunities. If that happens, Rwanda's young population becomes its greatest strength instead of its biggest problem.
