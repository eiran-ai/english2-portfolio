Cause and Effect Essay

Causes and Effects of Rapid Urbanization in Kigali

Kigali has changed fast. If you visited the city ten years ago and came back today, you would barely recognize some neighborhoods. New buildings, paved roads, shopping malls, and apartment complexes are going up everywhere. The city's population has been growing rapidly as people move in from rural areas looking for better opportunities. But this growth comes with both positive and negative effects. Understanding why people are moving to Kigali and what happens when they get there is important because urbanization is shaping Rwanda's future in ways that affect everyone, not just city residents.

The first cause of rapid urbanization in Kigali is the concentration of jobs and economic opportunities in the capital. Most of Rwanda's formal sector jobs, government offices, banks, tech companies, and international organizations are based in Kigali. Young people who finish secondary school or university in rural provinces often have no choice but to move to the city if they want salaried employment. The National Institute of Statistics of Rwanda (2022) reported that Kigali has the lowest unemployment rate in the country at 13.4%, compared to over 20% in some rural provinces. This gap pushes people toward the capital because even a low-paying job in Kigali can feel more stable than farming in a district where land is limited and crop prices are unpredictable.

The second cause is access to better education and healthcare. Kigali has more secondary schools, vocational training centers, and universities than any other part of the country. Families move so their children can attend better schools. The city also has the best hospitals, including King Faisal Hospital and Rwanda Military Hospital, which offer services that are not available in district hospitals. Muzzini and Lindeboom (2008) found that access to education and healthcare services is one of the main reasons rural families in East Africa choose to relocate to urban areas. I have seen this in my own extended family. Two of my cousins moved to Kigali specifically because the secondary schools near their village only went up to Senior 3, and they needed to finish Senior 6 to qualify for university.

One positive effect of this urbanization is economic growth. More people in the city means more customers for businesses, more workers for construction projects, and more demand for services like transportation, food, and housing. Kigali's economy has grown steadily, and the city now contributes a large share of Rwanda's total GDP. New industries like technology and tourism have also benefited from having a concentrated, educated workforce. The African Development Bank (2014) noted that urbanization in Africa, when managed well, can drive economic transformation by creating markets and encouraging innovation. Kigali's tech scene, with companies and startups operating in places like kLab and Norrsken House, is a good example of this.

But rapid urbanization has also caused serious problems. Housing is one of the biggest. As more people move to Kigali, rent prices keep rising. Many new arrivals cannot afford formal housing, so they end up in informal settlements on the outskirts of the city where living conditions are poor. Traffic congestion has also gotten worse. Roads that were built for a smaller population now handle far more vehicles and buses than they were designed for. Water and electricity shortages happen more often in fast-growing neighborhoods where the infrastructure has not caught up with the population. Lall et al. (2017) argue that rapid urbanization without matching investment in infrastructure leads to congestion, pollution, and inequality, which are problems that can undo the economic benefits of city growth. In Kigali, you can see this tension clearly. The city center looks modern and organized, but some neighborhoods just twenty minutes away still struggle with basic services.

Kigali's rapid growth is driven by real needs: jobs, education, and healthcare that are hard to find elsewhere. And the city has benefited from this growth in many ways. But the negative effects, such as rising housing costs, overcrowded roads, and strained infrastructure, are getting harder to ignore. If Rwanda wants urbanization to be a positive force, it needs to invest more in developing smaller cities and rural towns so that Kigali is not the only place where people can build a good life.
